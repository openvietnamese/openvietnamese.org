The Story of Chữ Quốc Ngữ

~1500s 1600s 1651 1600s–1850s 1880s–1920s 1945+ Today
Before the 1600s
Vietnamese had no alphabet of its own. Educated Vietnamese wrote in Chinese characters, or in chữ Nôm — a homegrown but very complex adaptation of Chinese characters for Vietnamese words. Reading and writing belonged to a small elite; the system took years even for native speakers to learn.
Early 1600s — missionaries arrive
Catholic missionaries — mostly Portuguese and Italian Jesuits — arrived in Vietnam to evangelize. To do that, they needed to actually speak with people, and eventually write religious texts converts could read without years of classical education.
The romanization effort
Missionaries wrote Vietnamese sounds using the Latin alphabet they already knew, borrowing spelling from Portuguese and Italian — and invented diacritic marks for something the Latin alphabet had never needed: Vietnamese's tones. Historians increasingly credit Francisco de Pina as the first to gain real fluency and begin this work — though Alexandre de Rhodes is the name most people know, thanks to his 1651 trilingual dictionary that standardized and published the system widely.
For centuries, a niche tool
This new script — chữ Quốc Ngữ, "national language script" — stayed mostly a missionary and Catholic tool. Chữ Nôm and Chinese characters remained the real literary standard for another 250+ years.
The turn
In the late 1800s, French colonial administrators promoted chữ Quốc Ngữ partly to weaken Vietnam's link to classical Chinese education. But Vietnamese reformers and nationalists — journalists, modernizers, independence movements — embraced the same script for the opposite reason: it was radically easier to learn than thousands of characters, which meant mass literacy was suddenly possible.
The payoff
After 1945, Hồ Chí Minh's government ran a nationwide literacy campaign — Bình dân học vụ — built entirely on chữ Quốc Ngữ's alphabetic simplicity. Vietnam's literacy rate rose dramatically faster than many neighbors still using logographic systems. Today it's the sole official script.
Back to this room
What you just did — guessing at spelling from sound — is a small, real echo of how this whole writing system got invented. You weren't just doing a warm-up. You were doing what actually happened.
